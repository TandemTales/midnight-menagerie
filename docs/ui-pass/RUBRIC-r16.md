# Blind style judgment — rubric, round 16: IS THE OBJECT FINISHED?

Read `RUBRIC.md`, then `RUBRIC-r9.md`, then `RUBRIC-r11.md` in full. The unit
is still a CONTACT SHEET of three rooms of one wing, and a sheet must win both
questions — are these different rooms, and are they the same wing? A change of
VANTAGE counts as a difference and belongs in `composition`. All of that
holds; this file wins where they disagree.

**Four sheets** — `sheet-foyer`, `sheet-ballroom`, `sheet-greenhouse`,
`sheet-graveyard` — plus `combat` at both sizes. The Bathhouse and the
Lampworks are not judged and no candidate was allowed to touch them.

## THE QUESTION THIS ROUND ADDS: is the object FINISHED?

Round 15 put frames on the walls, ceilings over the rooms and a beam across
the top of the frame. The judges' verdict on it was that the new objects are
framed but not finished — "smoke in gilt frames". So, on each panel:

1. **Pick the three nearest drawn objects and look INSIDE them.** A portrait
   has a sitter with a shoulder line, a collar and a hairline; a glass roof has
   glazing bars, purlins and a ridge; a plaster ceiling has a rose where the
   pendant hangs; a chapel wall has buttresses and a plinth; a conifer belt has
   trees of different heights, some with trunks. An object whose OUTLINE is
   right and whose INTERIOR is a smooth field is unfinished, and that belongs
   in `ornament` and `material`.
2. **A repeated element must vary.** One window repeated in two even rows is a
   pattern, not a building: look for varied bay widths, more than one window
   size, and some windows DARK among the lit. The same test applies to trees,
   balusters, niches and panels.

## THE DARKS ARE STILL THE HOUSE'S COLOUR

Round 15's winner raised its floor with a floored vignette rather than an
ambient, and a judge cleared it by measuring: its darkest decile was deeper
AND more saturated than the field's. Keep that test. **A candidate whose
darkest corners have gone pale, milky or grey scores BELOW a darker one**, and
say so in `worst_problem`; a dark room with nameable objects in it is the
target. Equally, a creature or a card that has lost its silhouette against a
newly-lit wall is a `readability` failure — the room serves the board.

## PERSPECTIVE AND FRAMING ARE CORRECTNESS

- A floor pattern whose unit does not shrink with distance, or that bends
  across the frame, is a drawing error — `material`, and name it.
- A room that does not fill its panel, inset in black margins while its
  neighbours bleed to theirs, is a `composition` defect, not a style.
- A cornice, impost or string course that does not share the room's vanishing
  point reads as a curved bay; say which line is wrong.

A deliberately bent or broken thing in a decayed house is none of these: the
test is whether a viewer can tell a leaning headstone from a badly drawn one.

## `fits_between_samples`

Unchanged: would a player moving through this wing believe it was a real house
with several rooms in it, rather than one set redressed? Three sheets in
sixteen rounds have earned it. Say plainly which panel costs a sheet its TRUE.

## `worst_problem`

Name the panel and the object, and say what is missing inside it. "The five
canvases on the landing wall are gilt frames with a pale oval where the sitter
should be" is useful. "The background is flat" has been written for sixteen
rounds and tells a builder nothing.

## What is NOT a difference any builder made

- The creatures, the HUD, the cards, the plates and the type.
- Which three room names a sheet uses, which is fixed for all candidates.
- Prop ARRANGEMENT within a panel, which machinery re-rolls per room.
