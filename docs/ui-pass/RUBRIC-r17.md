# Blind style judgment — rubric, round 17: PEOPLE, AND REPETITION

Read `RUBRIC.md`, then `RUBRIC-r9.md`, then `RUBRIC-r11.md` in full. The unit
is a CONTACT SHEET of three rooms of one wing, and a sheet must win both
questions — different rooms, same wing. `RUBRIC-r16.md`'s two tests still
apply: is the object finished INSIDE, and does a repeated element VARY. This
file adds one and sharpens the other.

**Four sheets** — foyer, ballroom, greenhouse, graveyard — plus `combat` at
both sizes. The Bathhouse and the Lampworks are not judged and were not to be
touched.

## THE PEOPLE IN THE HOUSE

This house is painted but unpeopled: its portraits, busts and gallery frames
have been empty or blank for sixteen rounds. Where a candidate has put a
figure in — a sitter in a canvas, a bust on a plinth, a silhouette behind a
gallery rail — judge it as you would any other object, and hardest at the
scale it is actually drawn:

1. **Is there a person there at all?** A pale lozenge on a dark ground is not
   a sitter; a smooth white ovoid is not a head. Name what you see.
2. **Is it the SAME person everywhere?** One sitter repeated across four
   canvases at one scale is a wall of prints from one plate, and it is the
   same defect as one window repeated in two even rows. Look for varied
   pose, scale, sex, age and direction.
3. **Does the face carry its three marks** — a brow, a nose line, one dark eye
   socket — at the size it is drawn? `mainMenu.png` carries them at 60 px, so
   the standard is set inside the samples.

Score this in `ornament` and `material`, and let it move `background` where
the figures are what the wall is for.

## REPETITION IS THE ROUND'S SECOND TEST

A repeated element that does not vary is unfinished work, whatever it is:
frames on a wall, fronds in a clump, conifers on a horizon, balusters on a
gallery, panes in a roof, windows in an elevation. Round 16's winner proved
the house can do it — unequal bays, more than one window size, some windows
dark among the lit, ragged tiers on the near trees. Hold every candidate to
that standard and name the object that fails it.

**A removed thing is a drawn thing.** An empty frame with a lighter, unfaded
patch of wall behind it is finished work; an empty frame with nothing behind
it is a placeholder. Tell them apart.

## AND THE TWO WAYS A CANDIDATE GOES BACKWARDS

- **Flattening.** Two of round 16's candidates smoothed away a coffered
  ceiling the baseline had drawn, while fixing something else. An object that
  was drawn and is now a smooth field is a REGRESSION: say so plainly and
  score it below the baseline on that screen.
- **Greying.** The darks are where this house keeps its colour. A candidate
  whose darkest corners have gone pale, milky or grey scores below a darker
  one, and a creature or card that has lost its silhouette against a
  newly-lit wall is a `readability` failure. The room serves the board.

## PERSPECTIVE AND FRAMING ARE STILL CORRECTNESS

A pattern whose unit does not shrink with distance, a room inset in black
margins while its neighbours bleed, a cornice that does not share the room's
vanishing point: drawing errors, named in `material` or `composition`. A
deliberately leaning headstone is not one — the test is whether a viewer can
tell a bent thing from a badly drawn one.

## `fits_between_samples` and `worst_problem`

`fits` is unchanged: would a player believe this wing is a real house with
several rooms in it? Three sheets in seventeen rounds have earned it.

For `worst_problem`, name the panel and the object and say what is missing
inside it. "The five canvases are gilt frames with a pale lozenge where the
sitter should be" is useful; "the background is flat" tells a builder nothing
and has been written for seventeen rounds.
