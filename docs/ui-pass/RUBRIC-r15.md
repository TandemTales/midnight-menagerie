# Blind style judgment — rubric, round 15: NAME EVERYTHING IN THE FRAME

Read `RUBRIC.md`, then `RUBRIC-r9.md`, then `RUBRIC-r11.md` in full. The unit
is still a CONTACT SHEET of three rooms of one wing, and a sheet must win both
questions — are these different rooms, and are they the same wing? A change of
VANTAGE still counts as a difference and still belongs in `composition`. All of
that holds; this file wins where they disagree.

**Four sheets this round** — `sheet-foyer`, `sheet-ballroom`,
`sheet-greenhouse`, `sheet-graveyard` — plus `combat` at both sizes. The
Bathhouse and the Lampworks are not judged this round and no candidate was
allowed to touch them.

## THE QUESTION THIS ROUND ADDS: can you NAME it?

Round 9 asked whether a viewer could name everything in the room. Round 14's
winner passed that in the centre of the frame and failed it at the edges, and
the judges said so on every sheet. So, on each panel:

1. **Walk the frame's EDGES and its UPPER HALF.** Is there an object there you
   can name — a cabinet, a chair, a hanging, a cornice — or does it fall into
   flat black above the fixture line and in the left and right thirds? Objects
   lost to darkness are a `background` and `readability` failure.
2. **But a LIFTED, GREY room is worse, and must score below a dark one.** This
   house's darks are where its colour lives, and a candidate that answered the
   note above by raising the ambient has undone the one measured advance this
   pass made in its rendering. The right answer lights the OBJECT — a candle
   beside it, a lit rim, a lighter material — and leaves the room dark. If a
   candidate's darkest corners have gone pale, milky or grey, say so in
   `worst_problem` and mark `palette` and `material` down.
3. **Is anything a PLACEHOLDER?** A rounded rectangle with a faint edge where a
   hanging, a picture, a reflector or a panel should be is an unfinished
   object, however good the room around it. Name it.

## THE HOUSE BEYOND THE ROOM COUNTS AS DRAWN WORK

Where a panel shows the mansion, a chapel, an outbuilding or a horizon, judge
it exactly as you judge the furniture: is the elevation built — a ridge, eaves,
reveals, sills, more than one window size, one dark window among the lit — or
is it a flat slab with a repeated window pasted on it? Does it change size and
position when the vantage changes, or sit at one size on one horizon in all
three panels? Score this in `background`.

## THE PANEL'S OWN FRAME

A room that does not fill its panel — inset inside black margins while its
neighbours fill theirs — is a defect in `composition`, not a style. Three
panels must read as three rooms, not two rooms and a photograph of one.

## PERSPECTIVE IS CORRECTNESS, NOT STYLE

A floor pattern that bends in an arc, blocks that do not converge on the
vanishing point, a pattern whose unit size does not shrink with distance: these
are drawing errors. Mark them in `material` and name them. A deliberately bent
or broken thing in a decayed house is not this — the test is whether a viewer
can tell a leaning headstone from a badly drawn one.

## `fits_between_samples`

Unchanged from `RUBRIC-r11.md`: would a player moving through this wing believe
it was a real house with several rooms in it, rather than one set redressed?
Three sheets in fifteen rounds have earned it. It is not a participation mark,
and it is also not out of reach — say plainly which panel costs a sheet its
TRUE.

## `worst_problem`

Name the panel and the object. "The mansion behind the railing is one arched
window repeated in two even rows on a flat slab" is the shape of a useful
answer. "The background is flat" has been written for fifteen rounds and tells
a builder nothing.
