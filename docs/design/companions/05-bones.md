# Bones, the Skeleton Puppy

_Source: Midnight Menagerie Design.docx, lines 3138-3830_

# Bones, the Skeleton Puppy
Bones is a playful recursion character whose body is also a resource system. His central puzzle is not simply putting cards into the discard pile and getting them back. He deliberately falls apart, turns his missing pieces into tactical options, loses access to some reliable actions while scattered, retrieves Tricks he has already used, hides important Tricks underground, then reconstructs himself for powerful payoff turns.
A weak Bones deck repeatedly retrieves its best card. A strong Bones deck treats the draw pile, discard pile, Buried zone, and Bones's own anatomy as one interconnected routing system.
## 1. Complete Mechanical Identity
### Overall gameplay identity
Bones is a zone manipulation and body state character.
His combat rhythm is cyclical:
Whole → Shed Bones → Rattle → become Scattered → exploit missing pieces → recover old Tricks → Reattach → become Whole → cash out → fall apart again
His recursion is deliberately constrained. Fetch gives individual Tricks a second life, but not unlimited repeated use. Burying offers deeper recursion and deck control, but it requires delayed gratification.
Bones rewards planning one or two turns ahead while maintaining an immediately understandable fantasy:
The puppy keeps losing his bones, burying things, finding them again, and somehow winning.
### Major strengths
Exceptional access to specific Tricks after they have been played.
Strong tactical adaptability because the discard pile functions as a secondary toolbox.
Excellent long combat scaling through repeated body transitions and Buried Tricks.
Strong ability to manipulate future hands.
Can deliberately thin his active deck temporarily by Burying unwanted Tricks.
Can turn spent cards into future resources instead of treating discard as dead space.
Several powerful burst turns become possible after carefully arranging Loose Bones, discard contents, and Buried Tricks.
### Major weaknesses
Fetching the same premium Trick repeatedly is intentionally restricted.
Burying Tricks sacrifices immediate tempo.
Many defensive Tricks are strongest while Whole, while many offensive Tricks prefer Bones to be Scattered.
Sitting permanently at maximum Loose Bones shuts down many Rattle engines because Bones can no longer Shed.
Constantly remaining Whole wastes many offensive and engine effects.
Poorly sequenced decks can draw several Reattach effects with no Loose Bones, or several Shed effects while already fully Scattered.
Short encounters can end before elaborate burial engines pay for themselves.
Bones has comparatively little straightforward passive scaling. Most scaling requires repeatedly doing something.
# Signature Systems
## Loose Bones
Bones starts each combat with 0 Loose Bones.
He can have a maximum of 6 Loose Bones.
Shed X: Increase Loose Bones by X, up to 6.
Reattach X: Reduce Loose Bones by X.
If Reattaching is listed as a cost, Bones must have enough Loose Bones to pay it.
Loose Bones disappear after combat.
Loose Bones represent how much of Bones's skeleton is currently detached from the rest of him.
### Whole
Bones is Whole while he has exactly 0 Loose Bones.
Some Tricks gain powerful bonuses while Whole.
### Scattered
Bones is Scattered while he has 4, 5, or 6 Loose Bones.
Some Tricks become considerably stronger while Scattered.
At 1 to 3 Loose Bones, Bones is neither Whole nor Scattered.
This middle range is intentional. Many decks want to move through it rather than simply choosing one permanent state.
## Rattle
A Rattle occurs whenever an effect actually causes Bones to Shed or Reattach one or more Bones.
Changing three Loose Bones at once causes one Rattle, not three.
An effect that attempts to Shed while Bones is already at 6 does not cause a Rattle because nothing changed.
An effect that first Sheds and then separately Reattaches causes two Rattles.
This creates a major distinction between:
Having many Loose Bones
and
Frequently changing how many Loose Bones you have
Those support different builds.
## Fetch
Fetch: Choose an eligible Trick in your discard pile and return it to your hand.
Fetching does not count as drawing.
When a Trick is Fetched, it becomes Slobbered.
Unless otherwise specified, restrictions referring to a Trick's Nerve cost use its normal printed cost rather than temporary cost modifications.
## Slobbered
A Slobbered Trick cannot be Fetched again during that combat.
It can still be drawn, played, discarded, or Buried normally.
Some specialized Tricks can remove Slobbered.
Whenever Slobbered is removed by one of Bones's effects, that Trick still cannot be Fetched until Bones's next turn unless an effect explicitly overrides this restriction.
This is the primary recursion limiter.
Bones usually gets to Fetch a particular Trick once. Extending that limit requires deck investment, delayed recycling, or Rare effects.
## Bury
Bury: Place a Trick into Bones's Buried zone instead of the draw pile or discard pile.
A normally Buried Trick receives 2 counters.
At the start of Bones's turn, each Buried Trick loses 1 counter.
When its final counter is removed, Dig Up that Trick.
Buried Tricks cannot normally be drawn, discarded, played, or Fetched.
Temporarily removing cards this way can therefore function as both delayed setup and temporary deck thinning.
## Dig Up
Dig Up: Return a Trick from the Buried zone to your hand.
Digging Up does not count as drawing or Fetching and does not make the Trick Slobbered.
A Trick that is Dug Up gains a Dug Up marker until the next time it is played.
Some Tricks receive bonuses when played with this marker.
If a Trick cannot enter the hand because the hand is full, place it on top of the draw pile. It retains its Dug Up marker.
A Slobbered Trick that is Buried remains Slobbered unless an effect specifically removes Slobbered.
## Temporary Trick: Spare Bone
Several Tricks create Spare Bone.
Spare Bone Skill 0 Nerve Choose one: Shed 1 Bone, or Reattach 1 Bone. Remove this Trick from combat after use.
Unplayed Spare Bones disappear at the end of the turn.
Spare Bone is intentionally simple. Its value comes from body positioning and Rattle engines rather than raw output.

These Commons establish five foundational packages.

Importantly, several useful Commons encourage movement between systems rather than committing immediately to one keyword.

### Why the Uncommons matter
This is where Bones begins specializing.
Rattle decks receive Jingle Collar, Off Leash, and Rattletrap.
Whole decks receive Heel!, Good as New, Pile of Me, and Tighten the Collar.
Scattered decks receive Scattershot Skeleton, Flop Over, and Spare Parts Everywhere.
Fetch decks receive Flying Femur, Boomerang Bone, Call That Back, and Scent Memory.
Burial decks receive Dug Up Dinner, Excavation Frenzy, Backyard Cache, Treat Stash, Skeleton Key, and Yard Map.
The bridge cards prevent these packages from becoming five isolated mini decks.
For example, Bury the Evidence converts Fetch exhaustion into delayed future value.
Good Boy! rewards either retrieval system.
Heel! converts Loose Bones into a Whole payoff.
Tail Going A Mile A Minute does not care whether the card came from underground or the discard pile.

# 3. Major Deck Archetypes
## A. The Rattle Engine
### Core strategy
Repeatedly Shed and Reattach small numbers of Bones to trigger effects based on changing anatomy.
The objective is not to reach 6 Loose Bones.
The objective is to keep moving.
### Important Tricks
Shake It Loose Reassemble Spare Parts Off Leash Jingle Collar Rattletrap Spare Parts Everywhere Skeleton Stampede Built Wrong, Still Works
### Strengths
Excellent scaling across longer turns.
Strong multi enemy pressure.
Spare Bones allow precise body positioning.
Can combine offensive and defensive actions because Reassembling often generates Guard.
### Weaknesses
Rattle engines can stall at either extreme.
At 0 Loose Bones, Reattach effects cannot trigger.
At 6 Loose Bones, ordinary Shed effects cannot trigger.
The deck therefore needs both directions.
A pile containing every Rattle payoff but too few body manipulation cards performs badly.
### Hybridization
Rattle naturally hybridizes with Scattered decks because repeatedly entering Scattered can trigger Spare Parts Everywhere.
It hybridizes with Whole decks because returning to Whole activates Tighten the Collar.
It also works with Fetch because Jingle Collar, Shake It Loose, or Reassemble can be deliberately replayed when the correct half of the engine is missing.
## B. Scattered Puppy
### Core strategy
Intentionally reach high Loose Bones and turn Bones's incomplete body into offensive pressure.
This archetype treats missing anatomy as a weapon.
### Important Tricks
Clatter Pounce Scattershot Skeleton Take Me Apart Flop Over Spare Parts Everywhere Every Bone at Once Headless Rush Play Dead Anatomy Is Optional
### Strengths
High burst damage.
Excellent multi enemy attacks.
Play Dead creates unusual defense against large enemy turns.
Several cards use Shedding as part of their offensive cost, making setup productive rather than passive.
### Weaknesses
Remaining at 6 Bones indefinitely is bad.
Further Shed cards become partially dead.
Many Whole cards stop functioning.
Reassembly remains necessary even if the deck is primarily a Scattered deck.
### Hybridization
A sophisticated Scattered deck often includes one major Whole payoff.
For example, it may spend several turns fighting Scattered, then use Pile of Me or Rebuild from Scratch to return to Whole and immediately use Good as New or Every Bone at Once.
This gives the archetype a cyclical finisher instead of simply rewarding permanent maximum Loose Bones.
## C. Fetch Toolbox
### Core strategy
Treat the discard pile as a second hand.
Bones uses ordinary Tricks once, then decides which of them deserves another appearance.
### Important Tricks
Fetch! Leave It! Shake Dry Flying Femur Boomerang Bone Call That Back Scent Trail Scent Memory Perfect Fetch One More Throw Favorite Toy Never Really Lost
### Strengths
Exceptional tactical consistency.
Can replay exactly the defense, attack, setup, or utility effect needed.
Medium sized decks become surprisingly reliable because spent Tricks remain strategically relevant.
Powerful against enemies whose behavior changes from turn to turn.
### Weaknesses
Slobbered prevents simply replaying the strongest Trick forever.
Fetch cards can become awkward early in a combat before useful targets reach discard.
Too many Fetch effects compete for too few eligible targets.
Heavy card draw is not automatically desirable because it can empty the discard pile faster than the Fetch engine wants.
### Hybridization
Fetch combines extremely well with Rattle because it can retrieve whichever body transition is currently missing.
It combines with burial through Bury the Evidence, allowing heavily used Tricks to take a slower route back into circulation.
Favorite Toy can turn one important bridge Trick into the centerpiece of the entire deck.
## D. Backyard Burial
### Core strategy
Use the Buried zone as delayed storage, deck thinning, tutoring, resource banking, and long fight scaling.
Bones intentionally removes Tricks from circulation and plans future turns around when they return.
### Important Tricks
Bury It Dig Here Under the Couch Backyard Cache Bury the Evidence Treat Stash Skeleton Key Yard Map Buried Bite Who Buried That? Secret Stash Dig to the Basement Treasure Yard
### Strengths
Excellent deck manipulation.
Very powerful delayed turns.
Can temporarily remove undesirable cards from the draw cycle.
Treat Stash and Treasure Yard turn future retrieval into Nerve generation.
Buried Bite gives the archetype genuine long combat inevitability.
### Weaknesses
The enemy does not care that your best Trick will be wonderful in two turns.
Burying too aggressively can leave Bones with a weak current hand.
Short encounters strongly punish slow burial setups.
Dig Up effects become poor draws if nothing useful has been Buried.
### Hybridization
Burial is Bones's most natural answer to Slobbered.
Bury the Evidence converts a Trick that has exhausted its Fetch privilege into a delayed reusable resource.
Burial also enables Scattered and Whole decks to hide cards that are currently incompatible with their body state.
A Whole payoff can be Buried while Bones becomes Scattered, then Dug Up precisely after Reassembly.
## E. Whole Dog Reassembly
### Core strategy
Exploit powerful effects requiring Bones to be fully reconstructed.
Unlike a traditional defensive archetype, Whole Dog is not about remaining at zero Loose Bones forever.
It repeatedly falls apart because several of its best effects require returning to Whole.
### Important Tricks
Tailbone Thump Sit, Stay Full Body Tackle Heel! Good as New Pile of Me Tighten the Collar Bone A Fide Missile Rebuild from Scratch Every Bone at Once Every Bone Knows the Way Home
### Strengths
Strong single target burst.
Reliable defensive turns.
Excellent conversion of Loose Bones into immediate output.
Produces satisfying payoff turns after several rounds spent Scattered.
### Weaknesses
Whole cards become mediocre when drawn at the wrong body state.
Maintaining zero Loose Bones permanently prevents the deck from accessing many of its strongest engines.
Repeatedly rebuilding can consume Nerve that more aggressive archetypes use directly.
### Hybridization
This archetype is deliberately designed to require another strategy.
Full Body Tackle and Every Bone at Once immediately destroy the Whole state that enabled them.
The player therefore needs Scatter, Rattle, Fetch, or Burial support to navigate back.
# 4. How the Archetypes Interlock
Bones should create a continual tension between state and motion.
A Scattered deck asks:
How many Bones am I missing?
A Rattle deck asks:
How often am I changing that number?
A Whole deck asks:
How efficiently can I return to zero?
A Fetch deck asks:
Which effect deserves a second use?
A Burial deck asks:
Which effect do I not need now, but will desperately want later?
Those questions frequently conflict.
If Bones remains Scattered, his Scattered payoffs work but his Rattle engine eventually stalls.
If he immediately Reassembles every turn, Whole cards work but Scattered payoffs never mature.
If he Fetches every powerful Trick immediately, he fills his discard pile with Slobbered cards and exhausts his future options.
If he Buries everything useful, he may simply lose before getting it back.
That friction is the character's strategic center.
# 5. Example Advanced Hybrid Deck
A sophisticated Bones deck might combine Rattle, Fetch, and Burial while using Whole as a periodic payoff rather than its primary identity.
Imagine a deck containing:
Jingle Collar Rattletrap Off Leash Shake It Loose Reassemble Flying Femur Call That Back Bury the Evidence Yard Map Treat Stash Heel! Bone A Fide Missile Treasure Yard
The player might begin by Shedding a few Bones and establishing Rattletrap.
Jingle Collar then produces two Rattles without permanently changing body state.
Flying Femur is played normally.
Later, Call That Back Fetches Flying Femur, turning its second appearance into a stronger attack.
Flying Femur is now Slobbered.
Instead of considering that card exhausted for the combat, Bury the Evidence removes Slobbered and places it underground.
While it is gone, Bones continues toward Scattered.
Eventually a Reassemble turn begins.
Treasure Yard rewards the return of the Buried card with Nerve.
Heel! Reattaches the final Bone and rewards becoming Whole.
Bone A Fide Missile converts the remainder of the reconstruction into damage.
The formerly Slobbered Flying Femur eventually returns to circulation and can be Fetched again on a later cycle.
That is the intended high level Bones experience.
The player is not executing a predefined combo. They are managing a circulatory system for cards and bones.
# 6. What Prevents Any One Mechanic From Being Universally Optimal
Loose Bones: High Loose Bones improve some Tricks but disable Whole effects and eventually prevent further Shedding.
Rattle: Rattle payoffs require continual access to both directions of body manipulation.
Fetch: Each Trick normally gets only one Fetch per combat before becoming Slobbered.
Slobber removal: Cleaning a Trick usually puts it back into the normal draw cycle or delays it, preventing immediate infinite recursion.
Bury: Powerful delayed manipulation always sacrifices immediate access.
Whole: Whole provides strong rewards but cannot generate meaningful Rattle or Scattered value without voluntarily abandoning that state.
Scattered: Scattered produces aggression and unusual defense, but permanently remaining Scattered eventually starves its own engines.
Spare Bones: These provide precise body manipulation but disappear if not used immediately, preventing stockpiling dozens of future Rattles.
# 7. Desired Player Experience
### A new player thinks:
“My puppy keeps falling apart. I can use the loose pieces, Fetch cards I already played, and put him back together when I need to.”
That is enough to make Bones functional.
### An experienced player thinks:
“I have three relevant card zones, a six point body state, two important thresholds, a limited recursion permission on every Trick, and several effects that care about transitions rather than states. I need to decide whether this card belongs in my hand now, my discard pile for Fetch, or underground for two turns, while making sure I can cross Scattered and Whole at exactly the right moments.”
That is the intended mastery curve.
