_Source: Midnight Menagerie Design.docx, lines 8343-9060_

# Pudding, the Graveyard Pug
Pudding is a tiny supernatural pug who has appointed himself the mansion graveyard’s official caretaker, night watchman, groundskeeper, mourner, emergency response team, and protector of absolutely everyone he likes.
He does not command the dead like a necromancer. He keeps watch over them.
His gameplay is about loyalty, safekeeping, deliberate burial, and choosing when to disturb the graveyard. Pudding can remove important Tricks from normal deck circulation by burying them in cemetery Plots, gain supernatural benefits from keeping those Plots occupied, then dig the Tricks back up at exactly the right moment.
His central strategic conflict is simple:
The graveyard is strongest when things stay buried. Your best Tricks are strongest when you dig them back up.
That tension should govern almost every sophisticated Pudding deck.
Costs below establish intended structural relationships, not final numerical balance. Damage and Guard values remain qualitative unless a quantity is mechanically necessary.
## 1. Overall gameplay identity
Pudding is a protective setup and retrieval character.
He has unusually strong control over where important Tricks are located. Instead of relying entirely on draw order, he can place Tricks into three special cemetery Plots and retrieve them later.
At the same time, occupied Plots make the graveyard itself more powerful.
His second major system is Loyalty. Pudding becomes more effective when enemies threaten the person he has decided is his responsibility. Loyalty can be stored for future turns and spent for emergency defense, explosive retaliation, or tempo.
In solo play, Pudding protects himself.
In multiplayer, he chooses a Best Friend, allowing his entire normal card pool to naturally become a support toolkit without requiring separate multiplayer versions of every defensive Trick.
Pudding is therefore neither a conventional tank nor a generic recursion character.
He is managing two things at once:
Who needs protecting?
Should this Trick stay buried, or do I need it now?
## 2. Signature mechanics
### Best Friend
At the beginning of combat, Pudding has one Best Friend.
In solo play, Pudding is automatically his own Best Friend.
In multiplayer, Pudding chooses himself or another player.
Many Pudding Tricks grant Guard or other benefits to his Best Friend rather than directly to Pudding.
If Pudding is his own Best Friend, an effect referring to both “you” and “your Best Friend” does not apply twice unless the Trick explicitly says otherwise.
Some advanced Tricks can change Pudding’s Best Friend.
### Loyalty
Pudding has a combat resource called Loyalty.
Maximum Loyalty is normally 5.
At the start of Pudding’s turn, if at least one enemy currently intends to Attack his Best Friend, Pudding gains 1 Loyalty.
This happens at most once from the basic Loyalty rule each turn, regardless of how many enemies are attacking.
Additional Tricks can generate Loyalty.
Loyalty persists between turns but resets between combats.
Some Tricks spend a fixed quantity. Others allow Pudding to spend any amount up to a specified maximum.
Loyalty creates a delayed reward for dangerous enemy turns. Pudding does not merely become defensive when threatened. He stores that tension and can turn it into future protection or aggression.
### Plots
Pudding begins each combat with 3 Plots.
Each Plot can contain one Buried Trick.
A Plot is either occupied or empty.
Each Plot can normally perform only one cemetery operation per Pudding turn. Once a Trick has been Buried in it or Dug Up from it, that Plot is considered used until the start of Pudding’s next turn.
Certain rare effects can override this rule.
### Bury
To Bury a Trick, move it from Pudding’s hand face up into an unused empty Plot.
A Buried Trick:
Is removed from normal draw and discard circulation.
Cannot normally be played.
Does not count as discarded.
Remains visible to the player.
Returns to Pudding’s deck normally after combat.
A Trick cannot normally be Buried and Dug Up from the same Plot during one turn because that Plot has already been used.
Some Tricks explicitly Bury themselves or Bury Tricks from unusual zones. Those effects override the normal hand requirement.
### Dig Up
To Dig Up a Trick, move it from an unused occupied Plot into Pudding’s hand.
The Plot becomes empty and used for the remainder of that turn.
A Dug Up Trick becomes Unearthed until the end of the current turn.
### Unearthed
Some Tricks have additional effects when they are played during the same turn in which they were Dug Up.
This is their Unearthed effect.
Unless another effect says otherwise, a retained Trick loses Unearthed at the end of the turn.
Unearthed gives burial a reason beyond deck thinning. Pudding can deliberately put a Trick away so that its eventual return is more powerful than an ordinary draw.
### Graveside
Pudding is Graveside whenever at least 2 Plots are currently occupied.
Graveside is checked at the moment an effect resolves.
This creates the defining strategic tension of the character.
If Pudding keeps two or three Tricks buried, his supernatural cemetery effects remain active.
If he digs those Tricks up for immediate use, he may lose Graveside.
## 3. Major strengths
Pudding has excellent control over important Tricks once his Plots are established. He can effectively reserve an answer for later rather than hoping to redraw it.
His defense is unusually reliable when enemies clearly threaten his Best Friend.
He can build toward powerful turns by storing both Loyalty and useful Buried Tricks.
He has strong hybridization because Loyalty, Unearthed, and Graveside frequently reward one another.
In multiplayer, much of his normal pool already functions as genuine support.
He is particularly good in encounters where threats alternate between dangerous attack turns and quieter setup turns.
## 4. Major weaknesses
Pudding requires setup. Burials consume hand resources and Plot operations before they become profitable.
Keeping Tricks Buried means voluntarily denying yourself access to them.
Digging too aggressively can deactivate Graveside.
Loyalty generation from Pudding’s innate rule slows down when enemies use buffs, debuffs, summons, or other non Attack actions.
His best offensive turns generally require preparation. He is less naturally explosive than a dedicated immediate damage Companion.
His unconditional area damage is mediocre. His better multi enemy tools tend to require Graveside or several occupied Plots.
In multiplayer, concentrating on one Best Friend means Pudding cannot effortlessly protect the entire group.
His cemetery mechanics can also punish poor planning. Burying the wrong Trick can be worse than simply drawing it later.
## 5. Recognizable archetypes
### The Loyal Guardian
Protect the Best Friend, accumulate Loyalty, then convert that Loyalty into efficient defense and retaliation.
### The Gravedigger
Deliberately Bury valuable Tricks, then Dig them Up for Unearthed bonuses and precise sequencing.
### The Haunted Cemetery
Keep two or three Plots occupied for as long as possible and build around Graveside Powers and passive supernatural effects.
### The Scrappy Watchdog
Use threats against the Best Friend as offensive fuel. Loyalty becomes damage, expensive Attacks become cheaper, and protecting someone is integrated into attacking.
### The Cemetery Caretaker Hybrid
Maintain two permanent Buried Tricks while using the remaining Plot as a rotating toolbox. This is likely to become one of Pudding’s highest skill ceiling strategies.
These archetypes intentionally compete for the same resources. The best Pudding decks should often combine them, but combining them requires managing their conflicting incentives.

### Common pool purpose
The Common pool establishes the entire Pudding puzzle without building the engines for the player.

Several Commons deliberately bridge mechanics.
Digging Claws connects Unearthed to Loyalty.
Fresh Flowers connects protection to Graveside setup.
Collar Charge turns Loyalty expenditure into offensive tempo.
Headstone Hop gives an aggressive deck a reason to maintain occupied Plots.

### Uncommon pool purpose
This is where Pudding begins splitting into real builds.
Hallowed Ground asks the player to stop excavating and maintain a stable cemetery.
Dog Eared Epitaph asks them to Dig constantly.
Those two plans can coexist, but only if the player carefully maintains at least two occupied Plots while rotating through another.
Cemetery Shift Supervisor also solves a weakness of Loyalty builds. It allows Loyalty generation during enemy turns where nobody threatens the Best Friend, but only if the player has invested in Graveside.
That is intentional cross archetype support.
Ghost Hound Charge is another explicit hybrid reward. A pure Loyalty deck or pure cemetery deck can reduce its cost somewhat, but fulfilling both conditions transforms it.

# STRATEGY ANALYSIS
## Archetype 1: Loyal Guardian
### Core strategy
The Loyal Guardian treats enemy aggression as a resource.
Pudding watches which enemies are targeting his Best Friend, accumulates Loyalty, and converts it into efficient protection.
Cards such as Stay Close, Good Dog Emergency, Never Off Duty, No One Gets Left Behind, and Stay With Me define this approach.
### Important mechanics
Best Friend
Loyalty
Guard
Enemy Attack intents
### Typical strengths
The deck is excellent against enemies with predictable heavy attacks.
It can bank Loyalty during manageable turns and save it for genuinely dangerous ones.
In multiplayer, Pudding can protect a fragile ally without needing that ally to draft defensive cards.
Many offensive cards also reward the same conditions, so defensive investment does not necessarily leave Pudding incapable of killing enemies.
### Typical weaknesses
Enemies that spend several turns buffing or applying statuses can starve Pudding's natural Loyalty generation.
A player who hoards Loyalty forever sacrifices tempo.
Pure guardian decks can also become too passive. Being difficult to kill is not enough against bosses with escalating mechanics.
### Hybridization
Cemetery Shift Supervisor supplies Loyalty from Graveside.
Crypt Keeper's Chomp turns Graveside into simultaneous offense and defense.
Good Boy of the Graveyard gives a Loyalty reward for successfully operating an Unearthed and Graveside engine.
The strongest Guardian deck therefore probably wants at least some cemetery infrastructure.
## Archetype 2: The Gravedigger
### Core strategy
The Gravedigger treats the three Plots as a second hand that requires planning.
Valuable situational Tricks are Buried before they are needed.
When the correct turn arrives, Pudding Digs them Up and exploits Unearthed bonuses.
Dig Here!, Dug Up Trouble, Double Dig, Keeper of the Yard, Exhumed Menace, Bury the Hatchet, and Exhume are major pieces.
### Important mechanics
Plots
Bury
Dig Up
Unearthed
Cost reduction
### Typical strengths
Exceptional access to planned answers.
Strong burst turns.
Can remove weak or currently irrelevant Tricks from draw circulation.
Can effectively reserve expensive Tricks until sufficient Nerve or supporting cards are available.
### Typical weaknesses
Burial itself does not win a fight.
A player can spend too much time creating an elegant cemetery while enemies simply attack them.
The three Plot limit creates real congestion.
Buried cards cannot help until retrieved.
Digging multiple cards at the wrong moment can leave Pudding with a huge hand and insufficient Nerve.
### Hybridization
Loyalty can fund a major excavation turn through Good Dog Reserve or Who's a Good Pug?
Graveside decks can keep two stable Buried cards and use the third Plot for active Unearthed cycling.
Cemetery Gates is the ultimate bridge because it allows retrieval while maintaining Plot occupancy.
## Archetype 3: The Haunted Cemetery
### Core strategy
This deck does something that initially feels wrong.
It buries good Tricks and deliberately leaves them there.
The goal is to remain Graveside and turn the occupied cemetery into a persistent supernatural engine.
Important cards include Hallowed Ground, Graveyard Rules, Cemetery Shift Supervisor, Little Ghost Escort, Graveyard Choir, Headstone Ricochet, and Cemetery Stampede.
### Important mechanics
Graveside
Occupied Plots
Powers
Burial
Passive effects
### Typical strengths
Excellent long fight scaling.
Once established, Pudding gains value without constantly spending cards to generate it.
Occupied Plots also shrink the actively cycling deck, allowing remaining Tricks to appear more frequently.
The archetype naturally supports both defense and area damage.
### Typical weaknesses
It sacrifices access to Buried Tricks.
It is vulnerable to short encounters because several turns may be spent establishing Powers and Plots.
Digging at an inconvenient moment can turn off several effects simultaneously.
The archetype also creates a subtle trap: burying three weak Tricks merely because they are weak may create Graveside efficiently, but those Tricks can become dead cemetery inventory when Pudding later needs the Plots for something valuable.
### Hybridization
Guardian is a natural partner because Hallowed Ground and Cemetery Shift Supervisor convert cemetery occupancy into protection and Loyalty.
Unearthed decks can use exactly two stable Plots and leave one Plot mobile.
Family Plot is particularly important because a fourth Plot dramatically reduces the conflict between maintaining Graveside and excavating important Tricks.
## Archetype 4: Scrappy Watchdog
### Core strategy
This is Pudding's aggressive interpretation.
The player deliberately converts being threatened into offensive momentum.
Bark First, Ask Later, You Heard Me Bark, Collar Charge, Leave My Person Alone, Ghost Hound Charge, Pug of the Baskervilles, and All Bark, Actually Some Bite turn Loyalty and enemy aggression into damage.
### Important mechanics
Loyalty
Enemy intent
Conditional costs
Multiple hits
Graveside support
### Typical strengths
Strong damage efficiency when enemies are aggressive.
Can turn a dangerous boss Attack into a turn where both defense and offense become more efficient.
Stored Loyalty allows damage to be delayed until a particularly important target appears.
### Typical weaknesses
The deck has worse efficiency when enemies are not attacking.
Spending all Loyalty offensively can leave Pudding unable to answer the next dangerous turn.
Several of its strongest Attacks are conditional rather than universally powerful.
### Hybridization
Guardian cards allow the deck to survive the attacks that create its offensive conditions.
Graveside helps Ghost Hound Charge and supplies extra Loyalty through Cemetery Shift Supervisor.
Unearthed Attacks such as Dug Up Trouble or Exhumed Menace give the deck a second burst axis that does not consume Loyalty.
# The intentional conflict between archetypes
Pudding should not have a simple answer to the question, “Should I Bury this?”
Consider a player with three occupied Plots.
They have Hallowed Ground, Graveyard Choir, and Cemetery Shift Supervisor active.
Keeping all three Tricks buried means:
More passive damage.
More protection.
Reliable Loyalty.
A thinner active deck.
But one of those Plots contains Bury the Hatchet.
Digging it up could produce massive immediate damage through its Unearthed effect.
Doing so also removes one occupied Plot.
If Pudding drops from two occupied Plots to one, Graveside turns off.
The correct decision depends on enemy Courage, incoming intent, current Loyalty, the contents of the hand, and what is likely to be drawn next.
That is the gameplay Pudding should repeatedly create.
# Advanced hybrid deck example
A high level Pudding deck might revolve around:
Cemetery Gates
Cemetery Shift Supervisor
Dog Eared Epitaph
Never Off Duty
Bury the Hatchet
Good Boy of the Graveyard
Good Dog Emergency
Who's a Good Pug?
Dig Until Dawn
The player establishes two mostly permanent cemetery residents.
Those two Tricks keep Pudding Graveside, supplying Loyalty through Cemetery Shift Supervisor.
The third Plot becomes a rotating utility Plot.
Cemetery Gates becomes extremely important because Pudding can exchange a hand Trick with the third Buried Trick without reducing the number of occupied Plots.
The retrieved Trick becomes Unearthed.
Playing it triggers Dog Eared Epitaph.
If it is Good Boy of the Graveyard, Pudding receives Nerve and Loyalty because he remains Graveside.
Spending that Loyalty triggers Never Off Duty, protecting the Best Friend.
When Loyalty reaches maximum, Who's a Good Pug? can convert the accumulated resource into a major draw and Nerve turn.
At the right moment, Bury the Hatchet is retrieved for its powerful second stage.
The sophisticated part is that the deck is not purely a Bury deck, a Loyalty deck, or a Guardian deck.
The cemetery maintains the Loyalty engine.
Loyalty finances excavation turns.
Excavation produces draw.
The resulting Tricks protect the Best Friend.
Protecting the Best Friend allows Pudding to survive long enough for the cemetery engine to matter.
# What prevents Pudding's mechanics from becoming universally optimal
### Bury is not free deck thinning
There are only three normal Plots.
Plots have operation limits.
Burying consumes card effects and potentially immediate tempo.
A Buried Trick is inaccessible until another effect retrieves it.
The strongest cemetery Powers also encourage leaving those Plots occupied.
### Unearthed is not automatically better than Graveside
Unearthed rewards excavation.
Graveside rewards not excavating too aggressively.
Many of Pudding's most interesting turns should involve choosing which one matters more right now.
### Loyalty cannot replace Nerve
Loyalty generally enhances Tricks rather than functioning as a complete secondary energy pool.
Only limited cards convert Loyalty directly into Nerve.
This prevents Pudding from simply stockpiling Loyalty and having enormous generic turns every combat.
### Guardian cards cannot solve every encounter
Many become strongest specifically when the Best Friend is threatened.
Enemy non Attack turns therefore reduce their efficiency.
In multiplayer, focusing on one Best Friend also creates vulnerability elsewhere.
### Graveside requires real commitment
Two occupied Plots means two Tricks are outside normal circulation.
Three means even less access.
Powerful Graveside decks gain substantial passive value, but they surrender flexibility to get it.
# Desired player experience
A new player thinks:
“Pudding protects me when enemies attack, and I can bury cards I want to save for later.”
That understanding is enough to play him competently.
An experienced player thinks:
“I need exactly two occupied Plots after this sequence. I can use the third for Dug Up Trouble, spend Loyalty on Good Dog Emergency to trigger Never Off Duty, then Cemetery Gates can replace the excavated Trick without dropping Graveside. If I wait one more turn, I can cash the cemetery out with Headstone Avalanche, but doing it now turns off Cemetery Shift Supervisor before the boss's next attack.”
That is the mastery target.
Pudding should initially feel like a very good little dog who guards important things.
At high level play, he becomes a resource routing character disguised as a pug, with the player continuously deciding what belongs in hand, what belongs underground, what must remain undisturbed, and who Pudding absolutely refuses to let the mansion hurt.
