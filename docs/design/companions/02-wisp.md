# Wisp, the Baby Will-o'-Wisp

_Source: Midnight Menagerie Design.docx, lines 1186-1830_

# Wisp, the Baby Will o’ Wisp
Wisp should feel like a tiny supernatural creature who is constantly saying, in mechanical terms, “Not yet. Not yet. Not yet. NOW.”
The central distinction is that Wisp does not primarily convert stored power into additional Nerve. Wisp converts time into power.
A weak Wisp deck plays ordinary Tricks and occasionally gets delayed bonuses. A strong Wisp deck deliberately arranges future turns, parks useful effects outside the deck, manipulates their arrival times, accumulates supernatural Glow, and decides whether to remain brilliantly charged or discharge that power for immediate survival or burst damage.
## 1. Overall gameplay identity
Wisp is a delayed resolution, timing manipulation, and stored power archetype.
Much of Wisp’s value does not occur when a Trick is played. Instead, Tricks can remain suspended in the supernatural space around Wisp before resolving later.
This creates three simultaneous questions every turn.
What do I need now?
What should I schedule for later?
Do I want my delayed Tricks to arrive separately for steady value, or simultaneously for a powerful Convergence?
Wisp gradually becomes more dangerous as Glow accumulates. However, maximum Glow is also a bottleneck. A player who refuses to spend it eventually wastes potential generation.
Wisp therefore oscillates naturally between charging, arranging, converging, and discharging.
## 2. Major strengths
Exceptional long fight scaling once delayed effects begin overlapping.
Can temporarily remove Tricks from the draw cycle by placing them in the Gloaming, allowing sophisticated deck compression.
Excellent ability to prepare large future turns.
Can convert otherwise inefficient setup turns into explosive Convergence turns.
Very flexible resource economy because Glow can support offense, defense, or utility depending on the deck.
Strong multi enemy potential through scheduled area effects.
Skilled players can manipulate timing to extract considerably more value from the same Trick pool than inexperienced players.
## 3. Major weaknesses
Wisp can be slow during the opening turns of combat.
Delayed defense is dangerous when immediate defense is required.
Poor scheduling can cause valuable effects to resolve when they are no longer useful.
Filling the Gloaming with too many Tricks can leave Wisp with an awkwardly thin active deck.
Hoarding Glow forever wastes future Glow generation once the cap is reached.
Aggressive Flare decks can accidentally spend themselves below important Bright thresholds.
Enemies that demand immediate burst damage can punish elaborate setup.
Hasten effects solve timing problems, but spending Tricks and Nerve to accelerate future value reduces the efficiency that made waiting attractive in the first place.
# 4. Signature mechanics
## Glow
Wisp begins each combat with 0 Glow.
Normal maximum Glow is 6.
Glow persists between turns but disappears at the end of combat.
Glow is not Nerve and normally cannot be used to pay Trick costs.
If Wisp would gain Glow above the current maximum, the excess is lost.
### Bright
Wisp is Bright while possessing at least 3 Glow.
Many Tricks become more efficient while Wisp is Bright.
### Blazing
Wisp is Blazing while possessing at least 6 Glow.
Normally this means Wisp is at maximum Glow, although certain Rare Tricks can raise the cap above 6.
Blazing represents Wisp reaching the point where stored supernatural energy is becoming difficult to contain.
## Linger X
A Trick with Linger X does not enter the discard pile normally after being played.
After its immediate effect resolves, place it face up in the Gloaming with X countdown counters.
At the beginning of each Wisp turn, all countdowns decrease by 1 simultaneously.
Any Lingering Tricks that reach 0 resolve their Afterglow effects, then enter the discard pile.
Afterglows are not considered Tricks being played.
They therefore do not trigger ordinary “when you play a Trick” effects unless explicitly stated.
## The Gloaming
The Gloaming is Wisp’s personal delayed Trick zone.
Lingering Tricks in the Gloaming are outside the hand, draw pile, and discard pile.
This creates an important secondary use for Linger.
Wisp can temporarily remove cards from deck circulation and deliberately alter the effective size of the deck.
## Afterglow
Afterglow is the delayed effect printed on a Lingering Trick.
If something causes a Lingering Trick to reach 0 during Wisp’s turn, its Afterglow resolves immediately after the current effect finishes resolving.
## Converge
If two or more Afterglows resolve in the same resolution batch, a Convergence occurs.
Any involved Trick containing a Converge effect receives that additional effect.
One resolution batch can create only one Convergence, regardless of how many Afterglows are involved.
If one effect Hastens several Lingering Tricks to 0 simultaneously, those Tricks resolve as one batch and can Converge.
Convergence rewards careful synchronization rather than merely playing as many Lingering Tricks as possible.
## Hasten X
Reduce a Lingering Trick’s countdown by X.
If it reaches 0, its Afterglow resolves.
## Delay X
Increase a Lingering Trick’s countdown by X.
Delay is not inherently negative. Many Wisp builds deliberately keep valuable effects suspended until they can be synchronized or amplified.
## Flare X
When playing a Trick with Flare X, Wisp may spend X Glow to activate the listed additional effect.
Flare is optional unless a Trick explicitly says otherwise.
This creates the character’s fundamental tension between remaining Bright and cashing out stored Glow.
## Vanish
A Trick that Vanishes is removed for the remainder of that combat after its relevant effect resolves.
# 5. Major deck archetypes
Wisp has five major strategic families.
## Convergence
Schedule several Afterglows to resolve together and receive powerful synchronization bonuses.
## Deep Gloaming
Keep several Tricks suspended at once, effectively shrinking the active deck while gaining bonuses based on Gloaming occupancy.
## Bright Wisp
Accumulate Glow and remain Bright or Blazing for persistent bonuses rather than spending it quickly.
## Flare
Repeatedly build and spend Glow for bursts of immediate value.
## Countdown Manipulation
Use Hasten and Delay effects aggressively, turning Wisp’s future schedule into something the player can actively rearrange.
The strongest Wisp decks generally combine at least two of these systems.
A pure Convergence deck may be too slow.
A pure Bright deck may waste Glow.
A pure Flare deck may constantly lose its own scaling.
A pure Gloaming deck may accidentally remove too many useful Tricks from circulation.
A pure countdown deck may manipulate effects without having sufficiently powerful effects worth manipulating.

# STRATEGY ANALYSIS
## 1. Convergence Wisp
### Core strategy
Build several Lingering Tricks and deliberately arrange their countdowns so that multiple Afterglows resolve together.
Cards such as Line Them Up, Same Time Tomorrow, All at Once, and Split Second turn the Gloaming into a scheduling puzzle.
### Important mechanics
Linger
Converge
Hasten
Delay
Gloaming management
### Typical strengths
Convergence decks produce extremely powerful prepared turns.
They are particularly effective in predictable boss fights because Wisp can arrange large bursts several turns in advance.
Convergence also allows one manipulation Trick to affect several future effects simultaneously.
### Typical weaknesses
The strategy can become too elaborate.
If an enemy is nearly defeated, having three major Tricks scheduled for two turns later is useless.
A sudden defensive requirement may also force Wisp to break the carefully constructed schedule.
### Hybridization
Convergence combines naturally with Glow because several Afterglows often generate large amounts of Glow at once.
It also combines strongly with Deep Gloaming because both strategies want several suspended Tricks.
Flare decks can use a Convergence as the charging phase immediately before a massive discharge turn.
# 2. Deep Gloaming Wisp
### Core strategy
Maintain several Tricks outside the active deck at once.
This produces a strange deckbuilding pattern where adding useful Linger Tricks can actually allow the player to create a smaller effective draw cycle during combat.
Orbiting Sparks, Still Here!, Room for More, Three Little Lights, and Gloaming Gets Crowded all reward this state.
### Important mechanics
Linger
Temporary deck compression
Gloaming occupancy
Delayed retrieval
### Typical strengths
Extremely consistent once established.
Removing four or five cards temporarily can cause Wisp to redraw the remaining engine pieces much more frequently.
This makes otherwise mediocre zero and one Nerve utility Tricks substantially more valuable.
### Typical weaknesses
There is such a thing as removing too much.
If the player suspends nearly every defensive Trick, the remaining micro deck may cycle beautifully while Wisp gets flattened.
It is also possible for several suspended Tricks to return to the discard pile simultaneously and suddenly make the deck much larger again.
### Hybridization
Deep Gloaming works exceptionally well with countdown manipulation.
It also creates natural Convergence opportunities because many Tricks are already waiting.
A Bright Wisp can use the Gloaming as a storage mechanism while benefiting from persistent Glow bonuses.
# 3. Bright Wisp
### Core strategy
Reach 3 Glow quickly, then maintain high Glow instead of repeatedly spending it.
Cards such as Saved Up Spark, Hallway Aurora, Bigger Than a Nightlight, and Too Bright for Bedtime reward Wisp simply for staying charged.
### Important mechanics
Glow
Bright
Blazing
Afterglow based charging
### Typical strengths
High consistency once charged.
Unlike Flare builds, Bright Wisp does not require a specific spender every time Glow is generated.
Its reward is an increasingly favorable baseline state.
Bigger Than a Nightlight dramatically changes this strategy by creating a second region above the original Glow cap.
### Typical weaknesses
Glow generation becomes worthless at the cap unless the deck has somewhere to put the excess.
This means a deck that simply grabs every Glow generator is poorly constructed.
Bright Wisp must still contain a few intelligent outlets.
### Hybridization
Convergence can rapidly move Wisp from 0 Glow to Bright or Blazing.
Selective Flare cards let Bright Wisp spend only enough Glow to avoid waste while remaining above the important thresholds.
# 4. Flare Wisp
### Core strategy
Treat Glow as a battery that repeatedly fills and empties.
Rather than staying Bright indefinitely, Flare Wisp converts Glow into immediate attacks, Guard, card access, and tactical effects.
Firefly Tackle, Emergency Lantern, Stash the Flash, Tiny Supernova, Blow the Fuse, and Never Goes Out are central examples.
### Important mechanics
Glow generation
Flare
Rapid recharging
Threshold management
### Typical strengths
Highly adaptable.
The same accumulated Glow can become offense in one encounter and survival in another.
Flare also gives Wisp better immediate combat performance than highly delayed builds.
### Typical weaknesses
Spending Glow can turn off Bright and Blazing bonuses.
An undisciplined Flare deck can repeatedly charge to 3, spend back to 0, and never exploit the stronger high Glow cards.
### Hybridization
Convergence provides excellent mass recharging.
Countdown builds can use Flicker Feedback to make spending Glow itself accelerate the next round of delayed effects.
Never Goes Out creates a particularly strong bridge between Flare and Bright because Wisp can spend while preserving part of the stored resource.
# 5. Countdown Manipulation Wisp
### Core strategy
Treat printed Linger values as suggestions rather than fixed delays.
The player continuously rearranges when effects will happen.
A Trick with Linger 3 might resolve immediately.
A Trick with Linger 1 might remain suspended for four turns.
### Important mechanics
Hasten
Delay
Convergence construction
Timing dependent payoffs
### Typical strengths
This is Wisp’s most tactically flexible archetype.
The player can salvage badly timed delayed defense, move burst damage forward, postpone effects until a boss becomes vulnerable, or deliberately create Convergences that were not originally possible.
### Typical weaknesses
Manipulation itself consumes deck slots and Nerve.
A deck containing ten scheduling Tricks and only two worthwhile Afterglows has successfully built an excellent calendar and forgotten to put anything important on it.
### Hybridization
Every major Wisp archetype benefits from some countdown control.
The important distinction is whether countdown manipulation is a supporting tool or the actual engine.
This One’s Been Cooking and Good Things Come to Tiny Ghosts create an especially unusual Delay focused version where making a Trick arrive later actively increases its eventual payoff.
# Advanced hybrid example
One particularly sophisticated Wisp deck could combine Deep Gloaming, Convergence, deliberate Delay, and Flare.
The player places This One’s Been Cooking, Spark Parade, and Darkest Before Dawn into the Gloaming.
Rather than immediately synchronizing them, the player uses Push It Back and Stretch the Moment to delay selected Tricks.
That delay produces defensive value through I Can Wait and improves This One’s Been Cooking.
The player then uses Same Time Tomorrow to place all three on the same countdown.
Meanwhile, smaller Afterglows have generated enough Glow to reach Blazing.
On the eventual resolution turn, all three delayed Tricks fire together.
The Convergence activates their relevant bonuses.
Good Things Come to Tiny Ghosts can repeat one deliberately delayed Afterglow.
Tiny Star, Long Shadow can repeat another.
The resulting Afterglows refill Glow.
The player can then immediately convert that stored Glow through One Bright Problem, Tiny Sun, Big Feelings, or a defensive Emergency Eclipse depending on the encounter.
The important part is that the deck is not merely a “Convergence deck.”
It has a setup phase, suspension phase, synchronization phase, recharge phase, and discharge phase, and the player can interrupt that plan at several points when circumstances demand it.
# Why the mechanics are not universally optimal
Linger produces delayed value and deck compression, but sacrifices immediate access.
Convergence provides exceptional efficiency, but requires multiple effects to become useful at the same time.
Glow hoarding creates strong Bright bonuses, but wastes excess Glow at maximum capacity.
Flare creates immediate power, but can disable Bright and Blazing effects.
Hasten solves urgency and synchronization problems, but sacrifices some of the natural efficiency gained by waiting.
Delay creates preparation opportunities and activates specific synergies, but can simply make useful effects arrive too late.
Deep Gloaming creates an exceptionally small active deck, but can remove the exact Tricks Wisp needs for the current enemy turn.
That tension is intentional. There should rarely be one universally correct answer to the question, “When should this light go off?”
# Intended player experience
A new player thinks: “I put my little lights away for later, they come back stronger, and when several go off together something cool happens.”
An experienced player thinks: “I need this defensive Afterglow in two turns, but I want the attack beside it to remain suspended for an extra turn because delaying it powers Good Things Come to Tiny Ghosts. If I Hasten the third Trick instead, I can trigger Gloaming Gets Crowded now, redraw Same Time Tomorrow, synchronize the whole cluster, enter Blazing from the Convergence, and convert the excess Glow before I hit the cap.”
That is the target mastery curve for Wisp.
Verification: 20 Commons, 35 Uncommons, 25 Rares, 80 regular Tricks total with continuous numbering from 1 through 80, plus 3 multiplayer Uncommons and 2 multiplayer Rares. All Wisp specific mechanics are explicitly defined, and the Basic starting deck is separate from the 80 card reward pool.
