# MM101 v3 — independent fresh child-oriented editorial review

Reviewed only `scripts/MM101-v3.fountain`, the complete supplied screenplay. No earlier draft, other review, score, or writer defense was consulted. This is an editorial simulation for an audience aged 7–10, not a report of actual child testing. Canon fidelity is therefore assessed only for the supplied brief and internal screenplay consistency; the source ledger was not needed or consulted.

## Verdict

**82.75/100. Revise before declaring the pilot ready.** The affectionate, mildly spooky world works. Maya, Mateo, and Mopsy each make consequential choices, and Mopsy's first little outing supplies a satisfying episode-sized success while the missing pets remain the larger problem. The major remaining obstacles are the escape sequence's geography and decision logic, followed by the unspoken reason the children leave immediately after hearing possible evidence of Orbit. Those are comprehension problems on the page, not matters an animatic alone can settle.

The comedy has several attractive visual premises, but the screenplay does not yet reliably promise the volume or strength of laughs implied by a genuinely funny adventure. The trolley sequence consumes considerable reader attention with handling instructions. Its actual screen duration remains unmeasured; no runtime has been inferred from word count.

## Findings and repair directions

### 1. Major blocker — the children appear to have an exit before the escape problem develops

**S09 → S10.** Mopsy lifts the cloth to reveal the familiar landing and service lift. Maya checks its level sill. Mopsy calls it “The way back,” and only the bottom of the hanging is stitched. The children then return to the Story Nook to argue with the Governess. For a child watching, the obvious question is: why not walk through the way back now? Maya's preference for explaining herself can motivate a mistake, but the episode must let us understand that choice and its cost. Currently the exit looks usable, and the group does not acknowledge choosing to leave it.

**Repair direction:** Make the exit's current condition and the group's immediate plan visible in one simple beat. For example, show that the stitched hanging cannot yet admit the children, then give Maya an explicit choice to try the main door while Mopsy prepares the return opening. Alternatively, let Maya knowingly choose to seek the key before leaving and have Mateo question that choice. Preserve an understandable reason to wait; do not manufacture a harsher threat merely to prevent an easy exit.

### 2. Major blocker — the trolley changes which side of the obstruction contains the children

**S10 → S11.** S10 says the children return into the Story Nook. The Giant then blocks the Story Nook arch and closet doorway. S11 places the Governess in the Story Nook and the children on the closet side of the folded frames, without staging the children's crossing. This matters because the entire solution depends on who can reach the closet, trolley brake, and return opening.

**Repair direction:** Establish one wide shot with three unmistakable landmarks: Story Nook, closet door, window bay. Stage the children's move into Bedtime Hall before the trolley blocks them, if that is the intended layout. Keep the camera-side description consistent across the scene break. A storyboard should illustrate the solution, not invent the missing relocation.

### 3. Major motivation gap — possible Orbit evidence does not cause an understandable decision

**S13 → S14.** Maya hears the three scratches and identifies a private behavior associated with Orbit. She marks a question on the map, and everyone leaves. The preceding escape gives them a reason to be cautious, but no one says why this new evidence does not warrant checking the bell gallery immediately. A young viewer invested in the cat could experience this as abandoning him at the moment they finally hear him.

**Repair direction:** Add a brief child-led decision that joins the clue to the next visit: they cannot locate the sound from the recording, their current route has led to confinement, and they need a better plan or help. Choose one clear immediate reason to stop and one concrete next step. Do not add a pet deadline. Preserve Maya's uncertainty about whether the sound proves Orbit's location.

### 4. Substantial pacing/comedy issue — the trolley solution has too many comparable handling beats

**S11.** Brake test, release, first push, stop, brake, tiny-bed claim, alternate-route assessment, release, corner call, pivot, pennant clearance, parking, final brake, and bag distribution compete for attention. The Giant selecting a tiny bed and rejecting the huge pillow is a good comic center. The surrounding movements risk turning the adventure into an equipment maneuver. This is a structural concentration problem even before anyone measures duration.

**Repair direction:** Retain Maya's command of the solution, Mateo's question, the Giant's tiny-bed preference, and the need to respect that preference. Compress the first failed trolley move and later repositioning into one visible problem and one coordinated solution. Put necessary access and safety detail into a concise staging note where possible while retaining safe on-screen action. The children should appear to solve a predicament, with the geography immediately readable.

### 5. Moderate comedy issue — several jokes share the same low-volume delivery

**Across S03–S13.** The coat sleeves, empty coat customer, interrupted bell greeting, bigger button, and Giant's disappearing bed have strong visual hooks. “Turn it over” revealing whiskers is particularly useful because children can instantly see why the offered care is absurd. In contrast, several beats rely on dry recognition: saving a blank page, the paper floor plan's corridor, a portrait looking historically important, “PLEASE DISTURB QUIETLY,” and the Butler's “A delivery.” They can be charming, but charm is not evidence of a laugh.

**Repair direction:** Preserve the strongest visual jokes and make two of them escalate or return with a changed outcome. Use a child's recognizable impulse—curiosity, impatience, attachment, excessive helpfulness—to cause the comic consequence. Avoid adding further unrelated house oddities simply to increase the count. Test the bell interruption and blanket accordion in boards for pauses and silhouettes; those are production timing questions after the story repairs.

### 6. Moderate ensemble issue — all eight participate, but some are easier to count than remember

**S01, S04, S07, S11–S12.** Maya leads and learns; Mateo investigates and changes the plan; Samir's politeness produces comedy; Lena's button attachment and watchfulness make a useful impression. Priya aligns, photographs, and spots corners; Eli has a precious screw and mechanical attention; Amina helps and receives Mopsy's first care. Those are beginnings, but the latter children's rescue actions mostly serve the same group function. A fresh viewer may remember “the other kids helping” rather than eight different people.

**Repair direction:** Give one or two supporting children a short, causally useful choice growing out of their opening behavior. Substitute this for a generic relay or clearance beat rather than adding eight introductions or eight speeches. Do not make the pilot carry sixteen full Companion introductions. The missing animals and house beings already remain legibly different on screen; the screenplay need not teach the entire roster at once.

### 7. Minor inference issue — the Butler's closing joke is harder to decode than the surrounding visuals

**S14.** Marmalade freely goes outside and comes back, usefully demonstrating a choice the Butler struggles to explain. His answer, “A delivery,” requires the viewer to infer his conceptual error and then infer why he stops elaborating. The action's thematic value is clearer than the joke.

**Repair direction:** Either let his visible reaction do the work or give the mistaken explanation a concrete visual consequence. Do not explain the theme at length. This is a polish issue, not a blocker.

## What the likely audience can follow and feel

**Entering:** Clear. The missing-pet search routes converge, Orbit's intact collar is physical evidence, and Pepper's recorded phrase opens the animal hatch. The children test the front door, which makes the first exploration feel chosen rather than inexplicably reckless.

**Leaving:** Physically clear at the front door; emotionally under-motivated after the scratches. The return path from the Nursery needs the repairs above.

**Maya's agency:** Strong. She proposes the joint search, checks access and exits, identifies the Coatcheck's behavior, retrieves bags, operates the trolley's brake and plan, and eventually makes room for Mateo's observation. Her crutches are part of how she moves and acts. No cure premise or disability punchline appears. Her repeated blocking of Mateo in S10 is a useful flaw, but the scene should avoid making her overlook an obvious usable exit solely to prolong that lesson.

**Mateo's agency:** Strong. Their initial evidence drives the visit; their coat experiment fails and creates a cost; their attention then discovers the distraction. They record the bells and ask both Mopsy and the Giant questions that change events. These are actions with consequences rather than designated clever dialogue alone.

**Mopsy's agency:** The emotional high point. She asks permission through her behavior, repairs only the broken strap, declines the closing task, opens the passage, chooses to cross, chooses to bring a blanket back, and chooses the umbrella room before outside. “If I can. I've got umbrellas to look at” gives her a life beyond helping the children. Her departure is not a replacement for finding the missing pets.

**Fear:** Suitable for the intended band on the page. Locking the Nursery door gives the care/possession premise real force. The Governess's sewing fingers stay away from skin, the Giant's preference is funny, and Mopsy exits by a real route. The Governess protecting the stitches is a valuable flash of care. Keep the threatened blanket barrier visually clear; overly languid handling could make the escape feel consequence-free, while predatory shot choices could make it harsher than the writing.

**Warmth and ending:** The red button, Mopsy's umbrella adjustment, and Maya eating while keeping Orbit's bowl provide concrete emotional images. The kitchen scene allows hope without falsely claiming a rescue. It will land more securely once the departure from the House is an intelligible decision.

## Revision versus production verification

**Resolve in the screenplay:** Early availability of the cloth exit; children's placement around the trolley; explicit reason and next step when leaving after the scratches; reduction of repeated trolley mechanics.

**Verify in boards/animatic:** Overall runtime; how many distinct laughs the visual gags earn; legibility of eight children in the escape; audibility of the original scratches without prematurely flagging them; a clear match when the recording replays; duration and intensity of the blanket threat; sufficient hold on Mopsy choosing and Maya's final bowl image.

An animatic may show that some prose-heavy handling compresses efficiently. It cannot by itself repair missing choices or contradictory starting positions. No finding here represents observed child comprehension or measured laugh response.
