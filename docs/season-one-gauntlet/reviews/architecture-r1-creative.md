## Structural and character findings

### C1 — High: Episode 8, Keeper releases the final lock

The children resist, redirect helpful systems, and then the Keeper releases the last lock. The architecture does not specify the new event that changes its decision. This risks a finale whose physical effort is exciting but whose moral decision happens because the story needs to end. Direction: build a visible test the Keeper can observe, including an animal freely leaving and returning or a resident freely staying while an exit remains available. The crucial observation must happen before the decisive release, with its feasibility established earlier. Do not make a promise of perfect outside safety the answer.

### C2 — High: Episode 6, Mooncake comes into view and is left behind

The explicit warning at the end recognizes the problem but does not fix the scene. A familiar pet is close enough to take a button; the children choose to continue instead of rescuing him. Respecting privacy is not a sufficient explanation for delaying a reunion with a pet held by someone else. Direction: show the actual physical limit before he appears (a one-way niche, partition, or wrong side of an opening), let Lena attempt an offered safe exit, and show Mooncake's own behavior. A privacy choice can govern contact, while an independently visible obstacle prevents departure. Keep the next objective causally tied to removing that obstacle.

### C3 — High: Episode 7, Jordan stops taking the next assigned rescue job

As written, the action could appear to leave an animal needing help so that Jordan can advance his own rescue. That reverses the intended lesson about caring without compulsory lifelong service. Direction: show the current animal safely settled and a resident choosing to take over, or reveal that the new assignment is redundant busywork. Jordan must stop the infinite obligation, not abandon an immediate need.

### C4 — High: Episode 8, Maya refuses to make Orbit ordinary

Maya's categorical refusal occurs before Orbit's choice and introduces an unestablished reversal offer. It could make the child decide which body the animal ought to retain, undermining the season's agency claim. Direction: remove the cure bargain unless essential, or have Maya refuse to bargain on Orbit's behalf rather than refuse the change itself. Do not imply supernatural transformation is always a required improvement or a shameful loss.

### C5 — Medium: Episodes 3–7 repeatedly secure passage by teaching the guardian a lesson

Groundskeeper allows records after patience; Archivist records an exception; Watcher permits passage after a bad prediction; Lamplighter redirects; Master gives up stage; Curator loans; Gardener lets go; Matron lends route; Harvest King accepts a different photo. Different visual skins conceal similar resolutions. Direction: select at least three encounters whose outcomes differ: a costly bargain that remains binding, a guardian who is bypassed and stays opposed, a failed plan that redirects the expedition, or a guardian helping for an unrelated self-interested reason. Preserve the clearer cases already present, such as the Confectioner's practical interest in correct deliveries.

### C6 — Medium: Episode 8, each child stops an old habit

The list is neat for the writer but risks becoming eight short thesis demonstrations. In particular, Priya putting down her schedule suggests that planning itself was the problem, despite source direction that a plan should adapt. Direction: make the crisis require a revised plan and explicit delegated choices. Two or three kids act in a scene while the others' growth is conveyed through practical contributions and later reunion behavior.

### C7 — Medium: Episodes 6–7 budget

Five minutes for Bathhouse and five for Pumpkin Grounds can accommodate a consequential short boss encounter, but not full introductions, an elite set piece, ordinary enemies, a companion relationship, a thematic correction, and a satisfying local ending. Direction: make these sequences serve the same accumulating operational problem (for example, establishing a traversable departure route), with one failure carrying across the regional boundary. Specify which boss is the episode's central opponent and which are meaningful secondary roles.

### C8 — Medium: Episode 3, living arrivals clarification

The safeguard is stated after the graveyard sequence, whereas the sequence initially presents a procession and only later reveals current pets are alive. Direction: put clear living-arrival evidence before the memorial scene, not merely an author instruction after it. Preserve an unhurried respectful memorial beat without demanding a frightening uncertainty first.

## Comedy and voice findings

### J1 — Medium: Episodes 5 and 7, compulsory celebrations

Both use an event whose host refuses the ending; both resolve when the children replace its required conclusion. The second will feel like a smaller repeat of the Ballroom. Direction: make the Harvest King a different comic problem: possession of the perfect group photograph, ceremonial transport logistics, or harvest inventory that has mistaken guests for produce. Choose one and escalate it physically rather than repeat the endless-party argument.

### J2 — Medium: Episodes 1 and 2 titles/premises

“Please Stay for Breakfast” gives the pilot a breakfast promise it barely fulfills, then “Breakfast Is Following Us” repeats the hook. Direction: retitle one around the concrete comic premise, such as check-in/bedtime for the pilot. The trolley can remain an evolving callback.

### J3 — Medium: Episode 4, theory-generated forms and labels

This is a strong opportunity for an actual comic scene, but the kids currently escape by describing behavior and identifying a bad prediction. Direction: supply a specific three-step gag escalation, a failed attempt caused by Mateo's enthusiasm, and one short sample exchange differentiating Mateo from Priya. Mark it as a sample rather than proof the whole dialogue pass is done.

### J4 — Low: Episode 3 tag, candle bows to trolley

The candle following the banner is understandable; bowing to a sandwich trolley currently looks like a free-floating oddity. Direction: establish that Jordan accidentally named the trolley the parade's guest of honor, or cut the bow and allow the quiet ending to stand.

### J5 — Medium: Episode 8 humor during action

The final hook gag is good. The climax itself loses the distinctive comic engine for a broadly described systems battle. Direction: retain one escalating hospitality complication that the kids intentionally repurpose, with a clear visual payoff. Hold laughter away from the actual pet reunions.

## Family co-viewing simulation

The strongest reason to continue watching is that each new region changes what the children know about care and captivity, through specific pet behaviors. The strongest reason to stop is that adults may anticipate the moral solution while younger viewers lose the practical objective among many named guardians. Keep the current destination and immediate task drawable in one shot. End each episode with something achieved, not simply permission to continue.

The companion voices remain a proposal. Limit a scene's active conversational participants; do not let a parade of guest companions each explain the local lesson. Silent animal business can carry the comedy and conserve dialogue.

## Gate decision

Revise C1–C4 before advancing. C5–C8 and J1/J3/J5 require substantive architecture changes, not a note saying scripts will solve them. Re-review actual revised material. The diagnostic score should remain below target until these causal and repetitive structures improve; a numerical increase alone does not close the issues.
